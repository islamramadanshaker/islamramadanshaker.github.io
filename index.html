Delivery Framework

Every project runs on the same spine

Scaled to its size, in this order. Skipping a stage does not save time — it moves the cost to coordination. I have worked hands-on through every stage below, and led the teams that delivered them.

01 · STANDARDS

Agree the rules before the first model exists

BIM Execution Plan, naming convention, coordinate system and the LOD / LOI matrix — settled with the consultant and the client while changing them still costs nothing.

02 · STRUCTURE

Decide what gets modelled where

The model register and the segregation strategy: which models exist, what each one contains, who owns it, and how they federate into a single coordinated whole.

03 · ENVIRONMENT

Build the Common Data Environment

Folder architecture, permissions by role and by company, and the WIP → Shared → Published → Archived route every piece of information travels, on Autodesk Construction Cloud.

04 · PRODUCTION

Let the authoring teams work

Worksets assigned by zone, shared levels and grids locked, ownership enforced by the register rather than by convention — so a team of eighteen does not collide with itself.

05 · COORDINATION

Federate on a schedule, not on a problem

The clash matrix decides which discipline is tested against which, at what tolerance and at what point in the programme. Clashes become tracked issues with an owner and a date; closure is evidenced in the model, not declared in a meeting.

06 · DELIVERY

Reviewed sheets and an auditable history

Shop drawings reviewed before submission, progress reported model by model against the client's own format, and a revision history that can answer who changed what, and when.

Case Study · King Salman University, KSA

Six thousand shop drawing sheets in six months

King Salman University is a multi-building campus in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. The structural shop drawing package ran to roughly 6,000 sheets, and the programme gave us six months to issue all of it — a thousand sheets a month, sustained, with no quiet weeks to absorb a slip.

We held a 95% first-time approval rate across that package. On a programme this compressed, rejection is the only thing that can really break the schedule: a returned sheet costs the drawing twice and the review cycle twice. Protecting the approval rate was the schedule strategy.

How it held

  • One source of truth. Every sheet came out of the LOD 400/500 Revit Structure model, so a change to the structure changed every drawing that showed it — no parallel set of CAD files drifting away from the model.
  • Coordination finished first. The federated model was driven clash-free in Navisworks against architecture and MEP before drawing production ran at full rate, so the team was detailing a structure that had already been resolved.
  • The team split by zone. Eighteen structural BIM engineers, with worksets and model ownership assigned by zone rather than by convention, so nobody waited on anybody.
  • A review gate that never moved. Every sheet passed my own review — accuracy, code compliance, constructability — before it left the office. That gate is where the 95% came from.
  • RFIs raised early, not late. Missing, unclear or conflicting design information was queried as soon as the model exposed it, so the answer arrived before the sheet was drawn rather than after it was rejected.

What the approval rate is worth, in sheets: at 95%, around 300 of those 6,000 drawings came back for revision. At 80% it would have been 1,200 — four times the rework, on a programme with nowhere to put it. The six months would not have held.
